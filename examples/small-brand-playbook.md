# Why Brands Matter

A brand is the feeling people carry away after they meet your business. It lives in the colours on your sign, the way you answer the phone and the story customers tell their friends.

Small businesses often think branding is something big companies buy. In practice the smallest shops have an advantage: every customer meets the founder, and every detail can be deliberate.

This playbook walks through the decisions that matter most, in the order you will meet them.

# The Five Foundations

Before you design anything, settle these five questions. Each one makes the next decision easier.

1. **Purpose**
   Why the business exists beyond making money. One sentence you would be proud to print on the wall.
2. **Audience**
   The people you serve best. Describe one real customer in detail rather than a vague demographic.
3. **Promise**
   What customers can always count on, whether that is speed, warmth or craftsmanship.
4. **Personality**
   Three adjectives that describe how the brand behaves. Use them to judge every piece of copy.
5. **Proof**
   The reviews, numbers and stories that show the promise is real.

## Finding Your Story

![A notebook with sketches of logo ideas next to a cup of coffee](image)

Every lasting brand has an origin story. Yours does not need drama. It needs to be true and specific: the moment you noticed a gap, the first customer who came back, the mistake that taught you what to stand for.

Write the story in under 150 words. Read it aloud. If it sounds like a press release, start again and write it the way you would tell it across a counter.

## Naming Without Regret

A good name is easy to say, easy to spell after hearing it once, and still fits when the business grows. Test every shortlisted name by saying it on the phone to someone who has never heard it.

Check the domain, social handles and trademark registers early. Falling in love with a name you cannot own is the most common naming heartbreak, and it is avoidable with ten minutes of searching.

Avoid names tied to one product or one street if you plan to expand. Descriptive names are easy to understand but hard to protect; invented names are the opposite.

When you have two finalists, live with each for a week. Write a fake invoice, a shop sign and a social post with each name. The one you stop noticing is usually the one that works.

## The Colour Decision

![A fan of paint swatches in warm reds and deep teals](image)

Pick one main colour that you can own in your category, one supporting colour, and a neutral for text. Three colours used with discipline look more professional than ten used at random.

> Choose colours for the feeling they create, then check that text on them is readable for everyone.

<!-- layout: two-column-2 -->
## Typography That Talks

![Printed type specimens pinned to a cork board](image)

Typefaces carry personality before anyone reads a word. A heavy serif feels established and trustworthy; a rounded sans feels friendly and modern. Choose one face for headlines and one for body text, and use them everywhere.

Set body text large enough to read comfortably on a phone and in print. Generous line spacing does more for readability than any font choice. Keep line lengths between 50 and 75 characters where you can.

## Your Voice in Writing

Your brand voice is how the personality adjectives sound in words. If one of them is "warm", that means contractions, short sentences and talking to one person at a time.

![A handwritten thank-you note tucked into a paper shopping bag](image)

Write a short voice guide with examples: a greeting, an apology, a sold-out notice and a thank-you. New staff learn more from four real examples than from a page of rules.

Read your website, your receipts and your email signature in one sitting. Anything that sounds like a different company is a candidate for rewriting this week.

<!-- layout: two-column-4 -->
## Consistency Checklist

Walk through every place a customer meets your brand: the sign, the website, packaging, invoices, uniforms, social profiles, the voicemail greeting and the delivery van.

For each one ask three questions. Does it use the right colours and typefaces? Does it sound like us? Would a first-time customer know it is the same business they saw last week? Fix the ones that fail in order of how many people see them.

## Measuring What Works

You cannot manage a brand by instinct alone. Pick a few signals and check them every month: how people find you, what they say in reviews, and how many come back.

## Launch Week

Plan the first week of a new brand like an event. Brief your team, update every profile on the same day, and tell your best customers first so they feel part of it.

Collect reactions as they happen. The questions people ask in launch week show you which parts of the story need to be clearer.

## Growing Without Losing Yourself

Growth tests a brand more than any competitor. New staff, new locations and new products each pull the brand in a slightly different direction. The five foundations from the start of this book are the anchor: when a decision is hard, go back to your purpose, audience, promise, personality and proof.

Hire for the personality adjectives as much as for skills. A new team member who naturally behaves the way your brand behaves needs far less training than a brilliant one who does not.

When you add a product, ask whether it keeps the promise. A bakery known for slow-fermented bread can sell coffee and stay itself; it becomes something else when it starts selling mass-produced pastries to hit a price point.

Revisit the brand once a year. Read your origin story, your voice guide and your consistency checklist with fresh eyes. Keep what still feels true, rewrite what does not, and resist the urge to redesign everything because you are bored of it. Customers rarely tire of a good brand as quickly as its owners do.

Finally, write down what you have learned. The notes you keep today become the playbook the next person running the business will thank you for.
